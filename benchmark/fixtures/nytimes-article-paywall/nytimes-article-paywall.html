The Quiet Restructuring of the Tech Hiring Pipeline

After a decade of growth-at-all-costs recruitment, large technology employers are rebuilding how they evaluate candidates — and shifting the cost of evaluation onto applicants in ways the industry has rarely confronted.

By Jane Almeida and Daniel Reyes
May 15, 2026 · 8 min read

For most of the past decade, the standard playbook for hiring software engineers ran something like this: a recruiter sourced you from LinkedIn, a phone screen with a hiring manager followed within the week, and a one-day on-site loop closed out the loop two weeks later. The candidate's time investment, before any decision was made, rarely exceeded ten hours.

That arrangement is now visibly under strain. Across more than two dozen interviews with hiring leaders at large U.S. technology employers conducted by The New York Times over the past four months, a consistent picture emerges: total candidate time-to-decision has roughly doubled, while the proportion of that time spent on unpaid take-home assignments has gone from a rounding error to, in some cases, a majority.

"We started asking for a small project — maybe four hours — in 2022 when the market shifted," said one director of engineering at a publicly traded enterprise-software company, speaking on condition of anonymity because the company has not made the change public. "By late 2024 the industry consensus was that you needed at least one substantial deliverable to filter on. Now we're routinely asking for twenty hours of work, distributed across two assignments, before we'll bring someone on-site."

Workers' rights advocates and a small but growing number of senior engineers say the practice has crossed an ethical line. The argument is straightforward: a take-home assignment is, in effect, free contract labor — labor that benefits the employer (in screening signal) at a cost borne entirely by candidates, most of whom will be rejected and receive no compensation for the hours.
